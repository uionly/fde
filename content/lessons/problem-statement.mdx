## Requests are evidence, not specifications

“We need a chatbot” tells you something about the stakeholder's proposed solution. It tells you almost nothing about the broken workflow, affected user, or cost of the status quo.

<FDEPrinciple variant="insight">
  Translate a requested feature into an affected user, a failing workflow, a baseline, and a target outcome.
</FDEPrinciple>

## A useful problem statement

A strong statement names **who** experiences **what problem**, in **which workflow**, causing **what measurable impact**, under **which constraints**.

<CustomerScenario>
  Northstar support specialists spend 31% of handling time searching across three policy repositories. The target is to reduce median resolution time without exposing manager-only procedures.
</CustomerScenario>

That statement is useful because it creates questions the team can answer. Which specialist groups experience the delay? How was the 31% measured? Which repositories and access rules are involved? What resolution-time improvement matters enough to change the workflow?

## Separate observation, cause, and hypothesis

Do not embed an untested diagnosis inside the problem. “Agents are slow because our model is too small” mixes a symptom with a proposed cause. Record the observable workflow problem first, then list competing explanations: retrieval vocabulary mismatch, stale content, permission filters, integration latency, or adoption friction.

A solution hypothesis should be falsifiable: “Permission-aware hybrid retrieval will reduce policy-search time for support specialists while maintaining zero unauthorized document exposure.” It remains a hypothesis until representative evidence supports it.

## Bound the first outcome

Name a baseline, target, observation window, and owner. Include non-negotiable constraints such as tenant isolation, response-time limits, and human approval. Avoid broad promises like “improve productivity” that cannot guide a release decision.

<Callout title="Problem-statement test">
  A team should be able to evaluate several solution shapes against the same problem statement. If the statement already dictates “build an agent,” it is still a request, not a problem definition.
</Callout>

Return the statement to users, operators, and the sponsor. Their disagreement is valuable evidence: it exposes workflow variation and conflicting success definitions before they become expensive architecture changes.
