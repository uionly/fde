## Threat modeling starts with the customer workflow

A list of model risks is not a threat model. Begin with the assets the customer cares about, the actors who interact with them, and the consequences of misuse. Then trace how data and authority cross system boundaries.

<CustomerScenario title="The support assistant has three kinds of power">
  Northstar's assistant reads private policies, searches customer accounts, and drafts refund actions. Employees, customers, source documents, third-party connectors, and administrators can all influence its behavior.
</CustomerScenario>

Map entry points, identities, data stores, model calls, retrieval indexes, tools, outbound destinations, and human gates. Label which inputs are attacker controlled and which actions are reversible.

## Write abuse cases as scenarios

Useful cases are concrete:

- A customer ticket attempts to change tool policy.
- A compromised connector returns fabricated authorization data.
- An employee searches another region's restricted cases.
- A stale approval is replayed with different arguments.
- A tracing system records full account numbers.

For each case, name preventive, detective, and recovery controls. Assign an owner and an observable signal. “The model should refuse” is rarely sufficient on its own.

<FDEPrinciple variant="warning">
  Threat model the data, identities, tools, and business impact around the model—not the model in isolation.
</FDEPrinciple>

## Turn threats into release evidence

Connect each important abuse case to a deterministic test or evaluation fixture. Verify tenant isolation at retrieval, authorization at tool execution, approval binding, egress filtering, rate limits, and incident alerts. Re-run these tests when prompts, models, connectors, or permissions change.

Review the model with customer security and operations teams. They know which records, actions, and outages matter most. The deliverable is a shared control plan—not a diagram that only the AI team understands.
